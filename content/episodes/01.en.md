Before explaining why Jarvis doesn't actually work, I should acknowledge how compelling the concept is.

"Jarvis, organise my schedule for tomorrow."

"Jarvis, what's wrong with this code?"

"Jarvis, I need to focus right now."

These conversations feel natural. And technically, current AI can handle all of them.

## So why doesn't it work?

The problem is moments like this.

Monday morning, 9am. Ten minutes before an important presentation. You slept badly, you're on your second coffee, and you've just found an error in your slides.

You say to Jarvis: "Read the situation."

> Jarvis reads the calendar, scans the files, parses the Slack messages. Returns accurate information. But doesn't know whether that information is what you actually need right now.

Pepper is different. Pepper figures out what state you're in first.

## Two layers of context

AI assistants deal with two layers of context.

**Informational context**: schedules, files, emails, codebases.

**State context**: what mode is the user currently in?

Jarvis is good at the first. But for AI to be genuinely useful, it needs to understand the second.

Are you in focus mode? Do you need to make a decision? Or are you just trying to organise your thoughts right now?

## Why this is hard

State context isn't communicated explicitly. Nobody says "I'm stressed right now." The signals are indirect.

Messages get shorter. Typos increase. The same question gets asked twice. Response wait times lengthen.

Reading those signals — that's the first problem Pepper is trying to solve.
